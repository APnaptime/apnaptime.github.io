Background

I wanted a 3D printer. In addition to a hobby, a home printer is a useful machine for "maker" style project and home DIY endeavors. My first printer was an $200 Ender 3 v2 back in 2022. I made several 3D printed PCB-less mechanical keyboards with that printer and did some slight modifications to it as well. In general that first Ender was not great - slow, hard to use and a pain to level and calibrate. I've since moved and gifted that old printer to my younger brother. He then notably got a different printer and I did much the same. My current printing hobby solution / home appliance for making physical objects is a Prusa Mini - a smaller footprint and build volume but much easier to use and service. I printed a few home improvement projects with it such as a set of shelf brackets for my now very small kitchen and some wall mounted coat hangers. It is a great printer, but I do find myself limited in the size of the objects I can make. It also has the unfortunate characteristic of being unenclosed resulting in being restricted to more basic filaments - sufficient for aesthetic and some light load bearing tasks but not enough for stronger and more exotic plastics.

Knowing that I wanted a printer, the question is now which one? This choice is usually influenced by a variety of factors. In a potentially particular order: availability, budget constraints, capability, durability, repairability, and fun factor. At the time of writing - August 2026 - most folks looking into a non-first filament style printer fit into 4 broad categories. First you've got the two big ecosystems / brands, Prusa (Prusa Research) and Bambu (Bambu Lab). Then the price to performance ratio market (Creality, Elegoo, e.g. the rest of the market). And lastly the DIY side where the hobby is the focus and how much is spent as well as the resulting performance is entirely up to the user (and the user's time investment); this segment covers projects such as Vorons, Annex Engineering, VZBots, Ratrigs and other such design projects / groups that grew out of the rep-rap movement (printers that print printers).

Going in order: Prusa is typically chosen for their robust history in the space with reliable printers, minimal tinkering, location of manufacturing (in Europe) and industry leading customer service / after sales support. Their documentation is incredibly accessible and combined with their design philosophy of printed parts and repairability focus - the machines are easy to maintain and source parts for. The downside of going with Prusas however is typically the cost to performance premium. Their machines typically aren't at the cutting edge as far as features are considered, and you'll be paying more for a comparably less capable machine. Despite those limitations, these would still be the machines I would recommend to most people who want to make things while also dipping their toes into the hobby. The main thing that Prusas do undeniably well is treading that thin line between easy to use but also teaching the user about the finer details of FDM (fused deposition modeling) printing just through daily use. These are the machines that I would fit into a classroom, teaching, or laboratory space. Current recommendation from Prusa: Core One

Next up is Bambu. They were a major disruptor to the consumer 3D printing scene back in late 2022 when they came out with the X1C - an inexpensive, enclosed, and fast printer with a modern interface and out of the box usability for even the most uninitiated. Those themes largely carry over to the current lineup of printers but with the addition of sensors and technology such as LIDARs, servo extruders, and "AI" print failure detection. While those marketed features span the gamut from less than helpful to genuinely ahead of it's time, as a whole, Bambu has been a positive driver for innovation and cost reduction in the consumer 3D printing space. The downsides associated with Bambus are somewhat poetically opposite of those of Prusa. While the machines themselves are comparatively inexpensive for the performance you get, the support and serviceability do leave a lot to be desired. Some aspects of their print failure software can not be disabled (or I haven't been able to figure it out - still not great) and has been a pain to work around which often leaves me wishing for a full manual mode. Data privacy is another emerging concerns as it does feel like Bambu is going down the Apple path with a fully integrated but closed source software ecosystem which has been great for the new user experience but is antithetical to the rest of the industry which has historically been open source and centered around hardware ownership. However, Bambu is still undoubtedly a cost-efficient option at the cutting edge of consumer 3D printing technology. The user experience and accessibility for the new maker is also second to none leading to a fitting reputation for Bambu printers as the "appliance" of the maker space - for those who want to be able to focus on the parts and not the printer. Current recommendation from Bambu: X2D + AMS Combo

I'm not too familiar on the rest of the market, but this is where you end up if you're looking up reviews and cross shopping based on prices. At the moment most of the companies in the market are aiming to nearly match the Bambu hardware trends while competing on price, or are aiming to fill niches not covered by the big two ecosystems previously mentioned. This is where recommendations such as the Centauri Carbons or the Snapmaker U1s live - usually offering near similar cutting edge features and performance at a fraction of the price but usually at the expense of ease of use and repairability. No recommendation from me for this section since I'm no longer as familiar, though it is where I started when I got my first printer back before Bambu came onto the scene as the default recommendation for those with limited budgets.

Lastly you have the project printers. Most of the printers in this category are user sourced and assembled in some capacity, but it's not a hard requirement, and I am in no ways an expert in this area so my categorization is somewhat unsubstantiated. From what I've gathered so far, most users looking at options in this category are already familiar with FDM printing and want a project that lets them tinker with the printer itself. The default and most common recommendation in this area are Voron printers centered around commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) parts with other mechanical components 3D printed. They have a pretty well documented build manual and have a large user base making this a safe first option for most people. The designs they've published are however on the lower end in terms of median performance. While it's true that the printer's performance is up to the user (though there are some crazy ones out there), the average and "baseline" speed and performance are lesser in comparison to other design projects in the space.
